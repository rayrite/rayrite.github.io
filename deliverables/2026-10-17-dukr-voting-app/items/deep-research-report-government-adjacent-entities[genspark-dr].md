### Reader's Guide

This document is designed for U.S. citizens seeking to understand the complex ecosystem of organizations and individuals that influence federal government operations without being formal parts of the three constitutional branches. The guide is organized into thematic sections covering campaign finance, lobbying, independent agencies, quasi-governmental entities, and civil society organizations. Each section includes definitions, legal frameworks, current data, and practical information about how these entities affect everyday governance. Where data conflicts or is incomplete, this is noted explicitly.

### A Note on Nonpartisan Framing

This guide attempts to present information neutrally, describing structures and documented activities rather than advocating for particular policy positions. Where controversies exist—such as debates over the proper scope of independent agency authority or the effects of campaign finance law—multiple perspectives are presented. Readers should note that some data sources themselves have institutional perspectives (e.g., advocacy organizations, think tanks with known ideological leanings), and these are identified where known.

## Part 1 — Foundations

### Why This Guide Exists

The American political system extends far beyond the three branches of government described in the Constitution. Between elections, between congressional sessions, and even between presidential administrations, a vast network of organizations shapes policy, influences regulation, and affects the lives of ordinary citizens. These "government-adjacent entities" include political committees that raise and spend billions of dollars, lobbying firms that advocate on behalf of corporate and organizational interests, independent regulatory agencies that wield significant enforcement power, quasi-governmental corporations that operate with mixed public-private characteristics, and think tanks that develop the policy ideas that eventually become law [Brennan Center for Justice](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/big-money-breaks-records-boosts-gop-5-things-know-about-midterms).

Understanding this ecosystem matters because these entities collectively determine which issues receive attention, how laws are written and enforced, and ultimately, how government resources are allocated. A voter who understands the role of a Super PAC, the structure of the Federal Reserve, or the lobbying disclosure requirements under the Lobbying Disclosure Act is better equipped to evaluate claims made by candidates, to trace the origins of policy proposals, and to hold power accountable [OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/outside-spending-2026).

The complexity of this system has increased substantially since 2010, following the Supreme Court's decision in *Citizens United v. FEC*, which transformed the campaign finance landscape by permitting unlimited independent expenditures by corporations and unions [FEC](https://www.fec.gov/legal-resources/court-cases/citizens-united-v-fec/). Since then, new categories of political spending vehicles have emerged, lobbying spending has reached record levels, and the legal status of independent agencies has been called into question by a series of Supreme Court decisions culminating in 2026's *Trump v. Slaughter* and *Trump v. Cook* [Supreme Court of the United States](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-332_new_geil.pdf) [Supreme Court of the United States](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25a312_5468.pdf).

This guide does not take a position on whether these developments are good or bad for democracy. Rather, it aims to equip readers with the factual and conceptual tools needed to form their own judgments.

### Definitions of Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **PAC (Political Action Committee)** | A committee organized under the Federal Election Campaign Act (FECA) that raises and spends money to elect or defeat candidates. Traditional PACs face contribution limits and must register with the FEC [FEC](https://www.fec.gov/press/resources-journalists/political-action-committees-pacs/). |
| **Super PAC** | An "independent expenditure-only committee" that may raise unlimited funds from individuals, corporations, and unions but may not contribute directly to candidates or coordinate with campaigns [FEC](https://www.fec.gov/press/resources-journalists/political-action-committees-pacs/). |
| **527 Organization** | A tax-exempt organization under section 527 of the Internal Revenue Code, organized primarily to influence elections; must disclose donors to the IRS but not necessarily to the FEC [Campaign Legal Center](https://campaignlegal.org/update/pacs-super-pacs-dark-money-groups-whats-difference). |
| **501(c)(4)** | A "social welfare" nonprofit that may engage in some political activity so long as it is not the organization's primary purpose; not required to disclose donors [OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/dark-money/basics). |
| **Dark Money** | Political spending by organizations that are not required to disclose their donors, typically 501(c)(4), 501(c)(5), or 501(c)(6) organizations, or funds passed through shell companies [USAFacts](https://usafacts.org/articles/dark-money-definition/). |
| **Lobbyist** | An individual who attempts to influence legislation or executive branch action on behalf of a client, and who meets registration thresholds under the Lobbying Disclosure Act [Lobbying Disclosure Act FAQ](https://lobbyingdisclosure.house.gov/FAQ.html). |
| **Federal Reserve** | The central banking system of the United States, consisting of the Board of Governors, 12 regional Reserve Banks, and the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomc.htm). |
| **Independent Agency** | A federal agency whose head(s) cannot be removed by the President except for cause, including the SEC, FTC, FCC, and others [Congress.gov CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11448). |
| **GSE (Government-Sponsored Enterprise)** | A financial services corporation created by Congress to enhance credit flow to specific economic sectors, such as Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac [FHFA](https://www.fhfa.gov/conservatorship). |
| **FFRDC** | Federally Funded Research and Development Center, a private entity with a unique relationship to a federal agency, such as RAND Corporation or MITRE [NSF](https://ncses.nsf.gov/resource/master-gov-lists-ffrdc). |
| **FARA** | Foreign Agents Registration Act, requiring persons acting as agents of foreign principals to register with the Department of Justice [DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/nsd-fara). |

## Part 2 — The Political-Spending Ecosystem

### PACs and the Campaign-Finance System

A Political Action Committee (PAC) is a committee organized under the Federal Election Campaign Act (FECA) that raises and spends money to elect or defeat candidates. The FEC recognizes several types of PACs, each with distinct rules [FEC](https://www.fec.gov/press/resources-journalists/political-action-committees-pacs/):

- **Connected PACs (SSFs):** Sponsored by corporations, labor unions, or trade associations; may solicit contributions only from affiliated individuals (e.g., employees or union members).
- **Nonconnected PACs:** May solicit contributions from the general public.
- **Leadership PACs:** Controlled by candidates or officeholders; may contribute up to $5,000 per election to other federal candidates.
- **Hybrid PACs (Carey Committees):** Maintain both a traditional PAC account (subject to contribution limits) and a non-contribution account for independent expenditures.

For the 2025-2026 election cycle (January 1, 2025 through March 31, 2026), PACs collectively raised $6.3 billion and spent $4.8 billion, according to FEC data [FEC](https://www.fec.gov/updates/statistical-summary-of-15-month-campaign-activity-of-the-2025-2026-election-cycle/). Congressional candidates raised $2.1 billion and spent $1.3 billion, while political party committees raised $1.1 billion and spent $824.8 million. Independent expenditures reported during this period totaled $252.1 million, with electioneering communications filings at $104,965.

A traditional PAC registered with the FEC must file regular reports disclosing its receipts and disbursements. Individuals may contribute up to $5,000 per year to a PAC, and a multicandidate PAC may contribute up to $5,000 per election to a candidate and $15,000 per year to a national party committee [Campaign Legal Center](https://campaignlegal.org/update/pacs-super-pacs-dark-money-groups-whats-difference).

### Super PACs and Independent Expenditures

Super PACs emerged from the 2010 federal appeals court decision in *SpeechNow.org v. FEC*, which applied the logic of *Citizens United v. FEC* to hold that outside groups may accept unlimited contributions from individuals and corporations so long as they do not contribute directly to candidates or coordinate with campaigns [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/citizens-united-explained).

In the 2026 midterm election cycle, independent expenditures from super PACs and other groups reached a record-breaking $2 billion through the end of September, a 51 percent increase over the last midterm cycle in inflation-adjusted terms [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/big-money-breaks-records-boosts-gop-5-things-know-about-midterms). This spending has been heavily skewed toward Republican candidates and causes. Two large dark money groups, American Action Network and One Nation, gave a combined $197 million to Congressional candidates on the Republican side [Politico](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/10/16/republican-super-pac-donors-musk-uihlein-yass-01122941).

Total midterm ad spending in 2026 is projected to hit $11.6 billion, up from $11.2 billion in 2024, with much of this money flowing through super PACs and 501(c)(4) nonprofit groups that spend on political ads and mailers without disclosing their donors [US News](https://www.usnews.com/news/politics/articles/2026-10-07/midterm-ad-spending-is-set-to-hit-11-6-billion-as-dark-money-grows). Corporate donations reached a record $646 million, with the crypto, betting, and AI sectors contributing $344 million—a 40% increase over the 2024 cycle [Reuters](https://reuters.com/article/2026-08-27) (snippet cited in search results, full URL not retrieved).

### 527 Organizations and 501(c)(4)s

**527 organizations** are tax-exempt entities organized under section 527 of the Internal Revenue Code primarily to influence elections. Unlike PACs, 527s are not regulated by the FEC but must disclose donors to the IRS. They are often used for issue advocacy and voter mobilization efforts that stop short of expressly advocating for or against a specific candidate [Campaign Legal Center](https://campaignlegal.org/update/pacs-super-pacs-dark-money-groups-whats-difference).

**501(c)(4) organizations**, commonly called "social welfare" organizations, may engage in political activity so long as it is not their primary purpose (generally interpreted as less than 50% of spending). Crucially, 501(c)(4)s are not required to disclose their donors to the public, making them a primary vehicle for "dark money" [OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/dark-money/basics). A 501(c)(4) may contribute unlimited funds to a Super PAC, further obscuring the original source of the money.

According to one source, 501(c)(4) nonprofits that don't disclose their donors reported less than $25 million in spending directly to the FEC, though this figure likely understates total political activity since such spending often flows through other entities [Facebook/Brennan Center](https://www.facebook.com/BrennanCenter/posts/super-pac-and-dark-money-spending-are-breaking-records-with-hundreds-of-millions/1607116484780143/).

### Dark Money

"Dark money" refers to political spending by groups that disclose their election spending but not the sources of their funds [USAFacts](https://usafacts.org/articles/dark-money-definition/). This spending typically flows through 501(c)(4), 501(c)(5) (labor), or 501(c)(6) (business league) organizations, or through shell companies and LLCs that contribute to Super PACs [SuperLawyers](https://www.superlawyers.com/resources/legislative-and-governmental-affairs/dark-money-campaign-finance-laws/).

Since the *Citizens United* decision roughly a decade and a half ago, dark money has grown substantially. One estimate suggests roughly $1 billion was spent through dark money channels in the decade following *Citizens United*, and 2022 contributions from dark money and shell companies were three times the 2018 cycle level [OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/dark-money/basics). For the 2026 midterms, more than $1 billion in dark money has already been reported, with ad spending overall projected at $11.6 billion [US News](https://www.usnews.com/news/politics/articles/2026-10-07/midterm-ad-spending-is-set-to-hit-11-6-billion-as-dark-money-grows) [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/big-money-breaks-records-boosts-gop-5-things-know-about-midterms).

A related pending case, *End Citizens United PAC v. FEC*, had oral argument in February 2025, but as of July 2026 no opinion had been issued—illustrating how campaign finance litigation can remain unresolved for extended periods [Election Law Blog](https://electionlawblog.org/2026/what-happened-to-end-citizens-united-pac-v-fec/).

**Important note on data consistency:** Different sources report somewhat different figures for total dark money and lobbying spending, reflecting differences in methodology and timing of data collection. OpenSecrets reported federal lobbying spending of $2.7 billion for 2026 (as of mid-year data), while another source (openlobby.us) reported a 2025 total of roughly $6.0 billion. These discrepancies are noted in the Data Limitations section below.

## Part 3 — Lobbying and Direct Advocacy

### The Federal Lobbying Industry

Under the Lobbying Disclosure Act (LDA), individuals and firms that engage in lobbying activities above certain thresholds must register and file quarterly reports disclosing their clients, issues lobbied on, and approximate spending. As of January 1, 2025, a lobbying firm is exempt from registration if its client's lobbying-related income is $3,500 or less per quarter, and an organization employing in-house lobbyists is exempt if its lobbying expenses are $16,000 or less per quarter [Lobbying Disclosure Act FAQ](https://lobbyingdisclosure.house.gov/FAQ.html).

Federal lobbying spending has reached record levels in recent years. OpenSecrets reports total federal lobbying spending of $2.7 billion for 2026 (data as of mid-year), across 46,704 filed LD-2 reports, with approximately 13,000 registered lobbyists [OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/federal-lobbying). The health sector leads all sectors in lobbying spending [OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/federal-lobbying/top-sectors).

Looking at 2025 data, one source reports total lobbying spending of approximately $5.13 billion (a 15.4% increase), with 13,715 lobbyists and 16,897 clients [OpenSecrets via search snippet, URL not directly crawled]. Another source (openlobby.us) reports an even higher 2025 figure of roughly $6.0 billion, with 23,545 unique lobbyists and about 38,000 clients—figures that appear to count lobbyists differently (e.g., counting unique individuals across multiple registrations) [openlobby.us, cited in search results].

The top lobbying client in recent cycles has frequently been the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, which spent $37.6 million on lobbying in 2026 according to OpenSecrets, with 147 lobbyists [OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/federal-lobbying/clients/us-chamber-of-commerce?client_id=161731&cycle=2026). Another source puts Chamber spending at $68.3 million from July 2025 through September 2026, covering transportation, trade, and aerospace issues [Capitol Receipts](https://www.capitolreceipts.com/lobbying/cchamberofcommerceofusa/chamber-of-commerce-of-the-u-s-a). For 2025 overall, the Chamber reportedly led all clients with $72.1 million in spending [OpenSecrets Facebook post, cited in search results].

Technology companies are also major spenders. In Q2 2026, Alphabet spent $5.3 million on lobbying (roughly $58,000 per day), and Microsoft spent at a rate of approximately $226,000 per day [OpenSecrets/about.bgov.com/legis1.com/legistorm.com, cited in search results].

### The Revolving Door

The "revolving door" refers to the movement of individuals between government positions and private-sector lobbying or consulting roles. Federal law (18 U.S.C. § 207) imposes restrictions on former government officials engaging in lobbying activities for a specified "cooling off" period, though enforcement and effectiveness of these rules are debated.

Recent data suggests the revolving door remains active. One source reports approximately 5,000 lobbyists with prior government experience among the roughly 23,545 unique lobbyists active in 2026—roughly 9 per member of Congress [openlobby.us, cited in search results]. A bill, H.R. 3554 in the 119th Congress, has been introduced to strengthen revolving-door restrictions, though its status as of October 2026 is unclear from available sources [congress.gov, cited in search results].

Academic research on the revolving door's effects is mixed. Some studies find that revolving-door lobbyists are more effective at influencing policy, while others find more limited effects. One 2023 study in *Political Research Quarterly* examined the policy consequences of revolving-door lobbying directly [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/10659129231177648). Another line of research, the "lobbying as legislative subsidy" theory, suggests lobbyists primarily provide information and drafting assistance to already-sympathetic legislators rather than changing minds [Hall & Deardorff, *American Political Science Review*, 2006, via Cambridge Core].

### Foreign Agents and FARA

The Foreign Agents Registration Act (FARA), administered by the Department of Justice's National Security Division, requires persons acting as agents of foreign principals to register and disclose their activities and financial arrangements [DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/nsd-fara). The FARA Unit's public office operates by appointment only, and registration statements are available online at fara.gov.

Enforcement of FARA has fluctuated over time. According to one 2025 review, the theme of that year was a "de-emphasis" of FARA enforcement—the Foreign Influence Task Force was disbanded, criminal charges were limited to espionage-like conduct, and the FARA Unit focused on civil enforcement [Saul.com 2025 annual review, cited in prior crawl]. A February 2025 memo from then-Attorney General Bondi reportedly limited criminal FARA enforcement [cited in prior search results]. A January 2, 2025 DOJ proposed rule sought to update FARA regulations [cited in prior search results].

There is ongoing debate about whether FARA should apply more broadly to think tanks and other organizations receiving foreign funding. One law review note examines the applicability of FARA to think tanks shaping U.S. policy [Columbia Journal of Transnational Law, cited in search results]. Proposed legislation H.R. 3966 in the 119th Congress would require disclosure of foreign funding above $10,000 per year to think tanks, but as of 2026 this bill remained unresolved [cited in prior search results].

## Part 4 — The Federal Reserve and Monetary Policy

### The Federal Reserve System

The Federal Reserve System is the central banking system of the United States, established by the Federal Reserve Act of 1913. It consists of:

- **Board of Governors:** Seven members appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate, serving 14-year terms.
- **12 Regional Federal Reserve Banks:** Located in major cities across the country.
- **Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC):** The monetary policy-setting body, composed of the seven Governors, the President of the New York Fed, and four rotating Reserve Bank presidents, meeting eight times per year [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomc.htm).

As of 2026, the FOMC membership includes Kevin Warsh (Chairman), John C. Williams (Vice Chair, President of the New York Fed), Michael S. Barr, Michelle W. Bowman, Lisa D. Cook, Beth M. Hammack (Cleveland), Philip N. Jefferson, Neel Kashkari (Minneapolis), Lorie K. Logan (Dallas), Anna Paulson (Philadelphia), Jerome H. Powell, and Christopher J. Waller [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomc.htm).

The Federal Reserve has a "dual mandate" from Congress: to pursue maximum employment and stable prices (often interpreted as 2% inflation) [Federal Reserve FAQs, cited in search results]. In September 2026, the FOMC raised the federal funds rate by a quarter point to a range of 3–3.75% to 4%, citing concerns about AI-related economic "shocks" and energy prices [NYTimes, cited in search results]. In October 2026, the FOMC voted 9-3 to keep rates steady [NYTimes, cited in search results].

There has been legislative effort to change the Fed's mandate. In May 2026, the House Financial Services Committee marked up H.R. 5396, which would replace the dual mandate with a single price-stability mandate [Congress.gov CRS IF12940, cited in search results]. The Fed's July 2026 Monetary Policy Report reaffirmed its existing strategy [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/2026-07-mpr-statement.htm).

### Recent Monetary Policy

The Federal Reserve's recent policy history includes:

- **2008 Financial Crisis:** The Fed engaged in unprecedented quantitative easing (QE) and emergency lending programs.
- **COVID-19 Pandemic (2020-2021):** The Fed cut rates to near zero and resumed large-scale asset purchases.
- **2022-2024 Inflation Fight:** The Fed raised rates aggressively to combat post-pandemic inflation.
- **2026:** As noted above, the Fed has been navigating a complex environment with AI-related economic disruption and energy price volatility.

### Fed Independence and Accountability

The independence of the Federal Reserve from direct political control has been a subject of intense legal and political debate, culminating in the Supreme Court's 2026 decision in *Trump v. Cook*.

**Trump v. Cook (2026):** President Trump attempted to remove Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook. The Supreme Court, in a 5-4 decision on June 29, 2026, denied the government's application for a stay of a preliminary injunction protecting Cook, effectively upholding the for-cause removal protections for Fed Governors [Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25a312_5468.pdf). Chief Justice Roberts, writing for the majority, emphasized that "not only the fact of independence but also the appearance of independence is key to the Federal Reserve's design," and that any change to this scheme must come from Congress, not the courts [Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25a312_5468.pdf).

This decision stands in contrast to the Court's ruling the same day in *Trump v. Slaughter* (discussed below), which struck down similar for-cause removal protections for FTC commissioners. The different treatment of the Fed versus the FTC reflects the Court's view that the Fed's unique role in monetary policy and financial stability warrants stronger independence protections [Harvard Law School](https://hls.harvard.edu/today/scotus-review-a-win-for-the-federal-reserves-independence/) [Yale Journal on Regulation](https://www.yalejreg.com/nc/the-uncertain-future-of-fed-independence-after-trump-v-cook/) [Brookings Institution](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/fed-independence-after-trump-v-cook/).

Academic research on Fed independence suggests that political pressure on the Fed can have real economic consequences. Studies have documented correlations between presidential rhetoric and Fed policy decisions, and historical research shows that periods of reduced Fed independence have been associated with higher inflation [Camous & Matveev, *CESifo Economic Studies*, 2021, via Oxford Academic] [Heckelman & Wood, *Economics & Politics*, 2005, via Wiley].

## Part 5 — Independent Regulatory Agencies

### The Independent-Agency Ecosystem

Independent regulatory agencies are federal agencies whose heads cannot be removed by the President except for specified causes (such as "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office"), rather than at the President's discretion. This structure is intended to insulate these agencies from direct political control.

Major independent agencies include:

- **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC):** Regulates securities markets.
- **Federal Trade Commission (FTC):** Enforces antitrust and consumer protection laws.
- **Federal Communications Commission (FCC):** Regulates communications.
- **Environmental Protection Agency (EPA):** Regulates environmental protection (note: EPA's status as an "independent agency" in the strict legal sense is debated; it is often classified as an executive agency).
- **Federal Reserve Board:** As discussed above.
- **Federal Election Commission (FEC):** Enforces campaign finance laws.
- **Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB):** Enforces consumer financial protection laws.
- **Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), National Labor Relations Board (NLRB), Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC), Food and Drug Administration (FDA)**, and others [various sources, including Reuters Practical Law and Wikipedia, cited in search results].

As of October 2026, the Paperwork Reduction Act lists 19 enumerated independent agencies, while the 2025 U.S. Government Manual lists 58 independent establishments [Reuters Practical Law, Wikipedia, Ballotpedia, cited in search results].

### The Unitary-Executive Debate

The "unitary executive theory" holds that all executive power must be under the direct control of the President, which would be inconsistent with independent agencies whose heads enjoy for-cause removal protection. This theory has gained traction in recent Supreme Court jurisprudence.

**Trump v. Slaughter (2026):** In a 6-3 decision on June 29, 2026, the Supreme Court held that the FTC's for-cause removal provision violates the separation of powers [Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-332_new_geil.pdf). President Trump had removed FTC Commissioners Rebecca Slaughter and Alvaro Bedoya in March 2025, stating only that their continued service was "inconsistent with [his] Administration's priorities" [Supreme Court opinion]. The Court ruled that because the FTC exercises executive power, its commissioners must be removable by the President at will, effectively overturning the 1935 precedent of *Humphrey's Executor v. United States* to the extent it protected agency heads from at-will removal [Congress.gov CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11448) [SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/trump-v-slaughter-2/).

This decision, issued the same day as *Trump v. Cook*, creates an apparent tension in the Court's jurisprudence: the FTC's removal protections were struck down, while the Fed's were upheld. Legal commentators have noted this creates uncertainty about which agencies will retain independence protections going forward [Consumer Finance Monitor podcast, October 15, 2026](https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2026/10/15/podcast-release-the-supreme-court-opinions-in-trump-v-slaughter-and-trump-v-cook-what-do-they-mean-for-the-future-of-the-administrative-state/).

**SEC v. Jarkesy (2024):** In a 6-3 decision, the Supreme Court held that when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud, the Seventh Amendment entitles defendants to a jury trial in federal court rather than an administrative hearing before an SEC administrative law judge [Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/22-859_1924.pdf) [Congress.gov CRS LSB11229]. This decision limits agencies' ability to use in-house administrative proceedings for certain enforcement actions and has implications for other agencies like FERC [Sidley law firm analysis](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2024/07/us-supreme-courts-jarkesy-decision-imperils-fercs-use-of-inhouse-hearings-to-impose-civil-penalties).

**Seila Law LLC v. CFPB (2020):** The Supreme Court held that the CFPB's structure—a single director removable only for cause—violated the separation of powers, though the Court severed the removal provision rather than striking down the agency entirely [The U.S. Constitution website](https://www.theusconstitution.org/litigation/seila-law-llc-v-consumer-financial-protection-bureau/). However, in May 2024, the Supreme Court ruled that the CFPB's *funding structure* (drawing from the Federal Reserve rather than congressional appropriations) is constitutional [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en-us/knowledge/publications/736842c4/supreme-court-rules-cfpb-funding-structure-is-lawful).

### Regulatory Capture

"Regulatory capture" refers to a situation where a regulatory agency, created to act in the public interest, instead advances the interests of the industry it is charged with regulating. The concept was famously developed by economist George Stigler.

Academic evidence for regulatory capture is mixed and contested. Some studies find evidence of capture in specific sectors (e.g., energy, securities regulation), while others find that agencies often act against industry interests [Datla & Revesz, *Cornell Law Review*, 2012, 547 citations, via HeinOnline] [Weingast, *Public Choice*, 1984, 1012 citations, via Springer] [Cox & Thomas, *Georgetown Law Journal*, 2018, 57 citations, via HeinOnline]. One line of research suggests that multi-member agencies may be less susceptible to capture than single-director agencies [Datla & Revesz].

The Cato Institute, a libertarian think tank, published a blog post arguing that the Supreme Court's recent decisions effectively end the "ninety-year experiment" of independent agencies [Cato Institute](https://www.cato.org/blog/supreme-court-ends-ninety-year-experiment-independent-agencies).

## Part 6 — Quasi-Governmental and Hybrid Entities

### Government-Sponsored Enterprises

Government-Sponsored Enterprises (GSEs) are financial services corporations created by Congress to enhance the flow of credit to specific sectors of the economy. The major housing GSEs are:

- **Fannie Mae (Federal National Mortgage Association)**
- **Freddie Mac (Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation)**
- **Ginnie Mae (Government National Mortgage Association)** — technically a government corporation within HUD, not a GSE in the strict sense
- **Federal Home Loan Banks (FHLBanks)** — a system of 11 regional banks
- **Farm Credit System**
- **Federal Agricultural Mortgage Corporation (Farmer Mac)** [FHFA](https://www.fhfa.gov/conservatorship) [Congress.gov CRS R46746] [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government-sponsored_enterprise) [BEA](https://www.bea.gov/help/faq/508)

**Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac Conservatorship:** Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac were placed into conservatorship by the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA) on September 6, 2008, under authority granted by the Housing and Economic Recovery Act of 2008 (HERA) [FHFA](https://www.fhfa.gov/conservatorship). As of July 2026, they remain in conservatorship—more than 15 years later [Congress.gov CRS R46746]. As conservator, FHFA has the powers of management, the boards, and the shareholders of the two enterprises [FHFA](https://www.fhfa.gov/conservatorship).

FHFA's 2025 Conservatorship Scorecard priorities are (1) equitable access to affordable and sustainable housing, and (2) safe and sound operations [FHFA](https://www.fhfa.gov/conservatorship). In 2025, FHFA announced 2026 multifamily loan purchase caps for Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac [FHFA news release, November 24, 2025](https://www.fhfa.gov/news/news-release/u.s.-federal-housing-announces-2026-multifamily-loan-purchase-caps-for-fannie-mae-and-freddie-mac). FHFA also scaled back affordable housing goals for 2026-2028, reducing the low-income home purchase goal from 25% [Legis1](https://legis1.com/news/fannie-mae-freddie-mac-affordable-housing).

Congress has periodically considered legislation to end the conservatorship. As of the available sources, a Republican lawmaker has proposed new legislation to establish a framework for ending conservatorship, but no such legislation has been enacted as of October 2026 [National Mortgage Professional](https://nationalmortgageprofessional.com/news/congress-weighs-new-roadmap-end-fannie-freddie-conservatorship).

Academic research on the GSEs has debated whether they were "creatures of regulatory privilege" that contributed to the 2008 financial crisis, or whether government policy more broadly (including the Community Reinvestment Act and broader housing policy) was responsible [various law review articles, cited in scholar_search results].

### Government Corporations

Government corporations are federal entities that operate like businesses but are owned by the government. Examples include:

- **U.S. Postal Service (USPS)**
- **Amtrak (National Railroad Passenger Corporation)** — an independent government-owned corporation with a presidentially-appointed, senatorially-confirmed board [Amtrak, cited in search results]
- **Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA)**
- **Export-Import Bank of the United States**
- **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)**
- **Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC)** [various sources]

The legal status and governance of these entities varies. Amtrak, for example, has a board whose members are appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate [Amtrak, cited in search results].

### Federally Funded Research and Development Centers (FFRDCs)

FFRDCs are private-sector entities that have unique, long-term relationships with federal agencies to perform research and development work that supports the agencies' missions. They are designed to operate with more flexibility than government employees while maintaining objectivity [MITRE](https://www.mitre.org/sites/default/files/2025-03/PR-25-0685-FFRDC-Primer-3-18.pdf) [IDA](https://www.ida.org/ida-ffrdcs).

As of February 2026, 12 federal agencies sponsor or co-sponsor 41 FFRDCs [Congress.gov CRS R44629]. Notable FFRDCs include:

- **RAND Corporation:** Operates four FFRDCs sponsored by the Department of Defense [RAND](https://www.rand.org/about/ffrdc.html). RAND was the first FFRDC, created by the U.S. Air Force in 1947 [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federally_funded_research_and_development_centers).
- **MITRE Corporation:** Operates six FFRDCs [MITRE](https://www.mitre.org/our-impact/rd-centers).
- **Institute for Defense Analyses (IDA)**
- **National laboratories** such as Sandia National Laboratories and Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory [NSF Master Government List](https://ncses.nsf.gov/resource/master-gov-lists-ffrdc)

FFRDCs play a significant role in shaping defense, intelligence, and science policy, though their influence is less visible to the public than that of lobbyists or think tanks. One doctoral dissertation examines threats to FFRDCs from foreign influence, particularly related to Confucius Institutes and other foreign-funded research entities [Liberty University digital commons, cited in scholar_search results].

## Part 7 — Civil Society and Policy Influence

### Think Tanks

Think tanks are research organizations that produce policy analysis and recommendations. They occupy a space between academia and advocacy, and their influence on policy is substantial but difficult to measure precisely.

Major U.S. think tanks include:

- **Brookings Institution** — generally center-left
- **Heritage Foundation** — conservative; known for "Project 2025"
- **Cato Institute** — libertarian; accepts no government funding [Cato Institute](https://www.cato.org/about/financial-information-funding-independence)
- **Center for American Progress** — progressive
- **Council on Foreign Relations (CFR)**
- **American Enterprise Institute (AEI)**
- **RAND Corporation** — often considered more academic/centrist [Top US Think Tanks 2026 ranking](https://topusthinktanks.com/)

The U.S. government has given at least $1.49 billion to American think tanks since 2019, though the vast majority of this funding goes to a small number of organizations [Quincy Institute](https://quincyinst.org/research/big-ideas-and-big-money-think-tank-funding-in-america/). The Think Tank Funding Tracker (thinktankfundingtracker.org, updated March 15, 2026) allows users to explore these funding relationships [Think Tank Funding Tracker](https://thinktankfundingtracker.org/).

There is ongoing debate about think tank transparency, particularly regarding foreign funding. One law review note examines whether FARA should apply to think tanks [Columbia Journal of Transnational Law, cited in scholar_search results]. A 2026 "On Think Tanks State of the Sector Report" examines how think tanks globally are responding to reduced funding, political pressure, and technological change [On Think Tanks](https://onthinktanks.org/sos2026/).

Academic research on think tank influence suggests they are most-cited by U.S. policymakers when they produce rigorous, data-driven work. The National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) is the most-cited think tank by policymakers, with 16,277 policy citations, followed by Brookings [Stimson Center, Quincy Institute, Think Tank Authority, cited in search results]. Think tanks influence policy both by advocating specific alternatives and by legitimizing certain policy approaches through their research [various sources].

**Note on Heritage Foundation and Project 2025:** The Heritage Foundation's "Project 2025" — a detailed plan for restructuring the federal government — has been a subject of significant political controversy. The available search results mention Project 2025 in connection with Heritage but do not provide detailed information about its contents or current political status as of October 2026.

### ALEC and Model Legislation

The American Legislative Exchange Council (ALEC) is a nonprofit organization that brings together state legislators and private-sector representatives to develop "model legislation" that can be introduced in state legislatures across the country [ALEC](https://alec.org/about/) [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Legislative_Exchange_Council).

At its 53rd Annual Meeting in August 2026, ALEC approved 74 new model policies, bringing its library to over 1,000 model policies. New policies include a Model State Artificial Intelligence Act and a Digital Asset Banking Act of 2026 [ALEC press release](https://alec.org/press-release/alec-announces-74-new-model-policies-on-critical-issues/) [ALEC article](https://alec.org/article/new-alec-model-policies-from-the-53rd-annual-meeting/). ALEC also published an AI Policy Toolkit in February 2026 [ALEC PDF](https://alec.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/ALEC_AIPolicyToolkit_2026.pdf).

ALEC's model-legislation process has been controversial, with critics arguing it allows corporate interests to effectively write state laws without public scrutiny, while supporters argue it provides valuable policy expertise to state legislators who may lack staff resources [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Legislative_Exchange_Council).

### Industry Associations

Industry associations and trade groups represent the interests of specific sectors of the economy. Major examples include:

- **U.S. Chamber of Commerce** — the largest business lobbying organization in the U.S., spending tens of millions annually on lobbying [OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/federal-lobbying/clients/us-chamber-of-commerce?client_id=161731&cycle=2026)
- **National Association of Manufacturers (NAM)**
- **Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America (PhRMA)**
- **American Bankers Association**
- **National Association of Realtors**

These organizations engage in lobbying, campaign contributions (often through affiliated PACs), and public advocacy. The U.S. Chamber of Commerce, for example, spent $37.6 million on lobbying in 2026 according to OpenSecrets, with 147 lobbyists registered [OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/federal-lobbying/clients/us-chamber-of-commerce?client_id=161731&cycle=2026).

## Part 8 — Synthesis

### Patterns Across Categories

Several patterns emerge from examining these government-adjacent entities together:

1. **Transparency gradients:** There is a wide spectrum of disclosure requirements, from the relatively transparent (registered PACs, registered lobbyists) to the opaque (501(c)(4) dark money groups, some FFRDC contracts, some think tank funding sources) [OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/outside-spending-2026) [Quincy Institute](https://quincyinst.org/research/big-ideas-and-big-money-think-tank-funding-in-america/).

2. **The money-influence nexus:** Campaign contributions, lobbying expenditures, and the revolving door are interconnected. A corporation or interest group may donate to a PAC, hire lobbyists who are former government officials, and fund a think tank that produces research supporting its policy positions — creating multiple channels of influence that reinforce each other [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/campaign-finance-scandal-whats-legal).

3. **Legal uncertainty and flux:** The legal framework governing many of these entities has been in significant flux, particularly due to Supreme Court decisions. *Citizens United* (2010) transformed campaign finance law; *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024) limited agency enforcement powers; and *Trump v. Slaughter* and *Trump v. Cook* (both 2026) reshaped the landscape of independent agency independence in conflicting ways [Supreme Court opinions, various CRS reports].

4. **Concentration of influence:** A relatively small number of entities account for a disproportionate share of spending and influence. A handful of Super PACs and dark money groups account for most outside spending; a small number of lobbying firms account for most lobbying revenue; and a small number of think tanks receive most government and foundation funding [OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/federal-lobbying/top-firms) [Think Tank Funding Tracker](https://thinktankfundingtracker.org/).

### Major Empirical Debates

Several empirical questions about the influence of these entities remain contested in the academic and policy literature:

1. **Does money buy policy outcomes?** Some political science research finds a strong correlation between spending and policy outcomes, while other research finds more limited effects, particularly when controlling for other factors [various scholar_search results, e.g., Kang 2016 in *Review of Economic Studies*; Gao 2022 in *Review of International Economics*].

2. **Is regulatory capture real and widespread?** Evidence is mixed, with some studies finding clear cases of capture and others finding agencies often act against industry interests [Datla & Revesz 2012; Cox & Thomas 2018].

3. **How effective is the revolving door?** Some research suggests revolving-door lobbyists are more effective, while other research finds more modest effects [McKay & Lazarus 2023, *Political Research Quarterly*].

4. **What is the true scale of dark money?** Because dark money, by definition, is not fully disclosed, estimates vary widely depending on methodology [OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/dark-money/basics) [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/fifteen-years-later-citizens-united-defined-2024-election).

5. **Does Fed independence improve economic outcomes?** Research generally supports the view that independent central banks produce better inflation outcomes, though the causal mechanisms are debated [various scholar_search results on Fed independence].

### Reform Landscape

A number of reform proposals have been advanced in recent years, though few have been enacted:

- **DISCLOSE Act:** Proposed legislation to require greater disclosure of political spending, including by 501(c)(4) organizations [referenced in handoff document, not independently verified in this research].
- **Honest Ads Act:** Proposed legislation to extend campaign finance disclosure requirements to online political advertising [referenced in handoff document].
- **"Audit the Fed" proposals:** Various proposals to increase GAO auditing of Federal Reserve monetary policy decisions [Federal Reserve/GAO relationship page, cited in search results].
- **Revolving door restrictions:** H.R. 3554 in the 119th Congress proposes stronger cooling-off periods for former officials [congress.gov, cited in search results].
- **FARA reform:** Proposed legislation (H.R. 3966) would require disclosure of foreign funding to think tanks above $10,000/year [cited in prior search results, status unclear].
- **FEC reform:** Proposals to restructure the FEC to break partisan deadlock, given its recurring quorum problems [various news sources on FEC quorum, e.g., CBS News, Ashurst Perkins Coie].

The prospects for reform are complicated by the fact that many of the entities discussed here benefit from the status quo and have the resources to lobby against reforms that would limit their activities [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/campaign-finance-scandal-whats-legal).

## Part 9 — Practical Tools

### Tools for Voters

Citizens can use several free, publicly available resources to research government-adjacent entities:

1. **FEC.gov** — The Federal Election Commission's website provides data on campaign contributions, PAC spending, and candidate finances. The "Raising by the Numbers" and "Spending by the Numbers" tools are particularly useful [FEC Data Portal](https://www.fec.gov/data/). Individual contributions over $2,000 can be searched [FEC individual contributions search](https://www.fec.gov/data/receipts/individual-contributions/?two_year_transaction_period=2026&min_amount=2000).

2. **OpenSecrets.org** — Operated by the Center for Responsive Politics, this site aggregates FEC data, lobbying disclosures, and information on dark money and outside spending. It provides easy-to-use tools for searching by candidate, donor, lobbyist, or organization [OpenSecrets](https://www.opensecrets.org/).

3. **Lobbying Disclosure Act databases** — The House Clerk and Senate Secretary maintain databases of lobbying disclosures at lobbyingdisclosure.house.gov and lda.senate.gov [Lobbying Disclosure FAQ](https://lobbyingdisclosure.house.gov/FAQ.html).

4. **FARA database** — The Department of Justice maintains a searchable database of foreign agent registrations at fara.gov [DOJ FARA page](https://www.justice.gov/nsd-fara).

5. **Federal Reserve data** — FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data) provides economic data, and the Federal Reserve website publishes meeting minutes, speeches, and policy statements [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/).

6. **FHFA reports** — The Federal Housing Finance Agency publishes conservatorship scorecards and reports on Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac [FHFA](https://www.fhfa.gov/reports).

7. **Think Tank Funding Tracker** — A tool for researching think tank funding sources [thinktankfundingtracker.org](https://thinktankfundingtracker.org/).

8. **Congress.gov** — Provides access to legislation, congressional records, and CRS reports [congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/).

9. **GAO reports** — The Government Accountability Office publishes audits and evaluations of federal programs [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/).

### How to Engage

- **Comment on proposed rules:** Under the Administrative Procedure Act, federal agencies must solicit public comment on proposed rules. Comments can be submitted via Regulations.gov.
- **Contact elected officials:** Voters can contact their representatives and senators to express views on legislation affecting campaign finance, lobbying, or agency structure.
- **Vote:** Ultimately, voters can support candidates who prioritize transparency and reform in these areas.
- **Support transparency organizations:** Organizations like OpenSecrets, the Brennan Center, and others rely on donations to continue their monitoring work.

## Data Limitations

This report has several important limitations that readers should be aware of:

1. **Conflicting lobbying spending figures:** Different sources report substantially different figures for total federal lobbying spending. OpenSecrets reported $2.7 billion for 2026 (mid-year data), while openlobby.us reported approximately $6.0 billion for 2025. These figures may use different methodologies (e.g., counting unique lobbyists vs. registrations, different time periods). Readers should consult both sources directly for the most accurate picture.

2. **Dark money estimates are inherently uncertain:** By definition, dark money is not fully disclosed, so all figures are estimates based on partial data. Different organizations (OpenSecrets, Brennan Center, etc.) use different methodologies, leading to different estimates.

3. **FEC quorum status is unclear/contradictory across sources:** Some sources state the FEC regained quorum in February 2026 [Congress.gov CRS R45160], while others state it has been without quorum since April 2025 [Ballotpedia, CBS News, Ashurst Perkins Coie]. This discrepancy could not be resolved with the available sources and should be verified directly with the FEC or a more authoritative source.

4. **Some Supreme Court case details are incomplete:** While the outcomes of *Trump v. Slaughter* and *Trump v. Cook* are reasonably well-documented, the full reasoning and dissenting opinions were not fully extracted in all cases, and the longer-term implications remain uncertain and are the subject of ongoing legal commentary.

5. **Think tank funding data is incomplete:** The Think Tank Funding Tracker and other sources provide partial data, but comprehensive, verified figures on think tank funding (especially from opaque sources like donor-advised funds) are not available.

6. **Several URLs could not be crawled successfully:** The crawler failed to retrieve content from https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/fedexplained.htm and several Supreme Court opinion PDFs returned limited or no extractable text. This means some details about Fed structure and the full text of certain Court opinions are not fully represented in this report.

7. **Some 2026 data is preliminary:** Because the research was conducted in October 2026, many 2026 figures (lobbying, campaign spending) are year-to-date and not full-year totals.

8. **The original handoff document's "10 stopping criteria" were not found as a discrete list** — the research identified "completion criteria" embedded in the 14 workstreams instead, which may not fully match what the original handoff intended.

9. **Several scholar_search results had incomplete metadata** (missing authors, publication dates, or full abstracts), limiting the ability to accurately characterize the academic literature in some areas.

Readers seeking the most current and precise information should consult the primary sources listed in the Reference section directly, as some figures and legal statuses may have changed since the sources were accessed.

## Conclusion

The ecosystem of government-adjacent entities in the United States is vast, complex, and constantly evolving. From the billions of dollars flowing through Super PACs and dark money channels, to the thousands of registered lobbyists shaping legislation, to the independent agencies whose legal status has been thrown into question by recent Supreme Court decisions, these entities exert enormous influence on American governance — often with limited transparency or accountability.

For citizens seeking to understand and engage with this system, the key takeaways are:

1. **Follow the money** — Tools like OpenSecrets and FEC.gov make it possible to trace much (though not all) political spending.
2. **Understand the legal framework** — Campaign finance law, lobbying disclosure rules, and administrative law are technical but crucial to understanding how influence operates.
3. **Recognize the limits of transparency** — Dark money, the revolving door, and think tank funding often operate in shadows that are difficult to penetrate even with public data.
4. **Stay informed about legal developments** — The Supreme Court's recent decisions on independent agencies (*Trump v. Slaughter*, *Trump v. Cook*, *SEC v. Jarkesy*) have significantly reshaped the legal landscape, and further changes are likely.
5. **Engage through available channels** — Commenting on agency rules, contacting representatives, and supporting transparency organizations are all ways citizens can participate.

This guide is intended as a starting point, not a definitive account. The landscape of government-adjacent influence is dynamic, and readers are encouraged to consult the primary sources cited throughout this document for the most current information.
